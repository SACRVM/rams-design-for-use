# The ten principles applied to operation

Each principle opens with a paraphrase of Rams' text; text in quotation marks is his wording. The rest is this skill's application to how people operate software, apps, tools and games alike, not Rams' words. Rules already in `SKILL.md` are not repeated; this file adds the failure patterns.

### 1. Good design is innovative
Rams: innovative design develops together with new technology and new capabilities; it is never an end in itself.
- New ways of operating are justified only when they let people do something better than the familiar way. Otherwise use what they already know.
- Rethink the task before adding features: could it need fewer steps altogether?
- Failure: reinvented controls that behave worse than the platform's, unusual menus or control schemes people must relearn, gesture-only navigation.

### 2. Good design makes a product useful
Rams: a product is bought to be used. It must satisfy functional, psychological and aesthetic criteria; good design emphasizes usefulness and leaves out anything that detracts from it.
- The common case needs the fewest decisions: good defaults, repeated work done in bulk, a quick start.
- Nothing stands between people and what they came for (in a game, the challenge itself is what they came for).
- Failure: forced waits, unskippable sequences, slow menus, setup before any value.

### 3. Good design is aesthetic
Rams: aesthetic quality is part of usefulness, because people live with products every day. "Only well-executed objects can be beautiful."
- Order helps operation: a consistent rhythm, few type styles, a clear hierarchy of what matters.
- Atmosphere and style are welcome as long as they never cost legibility or speed.
- Failure: mixed symbol styles, decoration that competes with content, animation people have to wait for.

### 4. Good design makes a product understandable
Rams: it clarifies the product's structure; at best, it explains itself.
- In software the self-explaining part is labels, symbols, data display and wording (at Braun it was the scales and lettering on the device).
- Teach by doing: one thing at a time, in context, and get out of the way once it's learned.
- Failure: symbols without names, modes people can't see they're in, a command or key that gives no confirmation, a manual or tutorial that compensates for an unclear design.

### 5. Good design is unobtrusive
Rams: products that serve a purpose are like tools, neither decoration nor works of art. Their design is neutral and restrained, to leave room for the user's self-expression.
- The person's content, goal or play is the hero; the interface steps back.
- Interrupt only when it matters to them right now. Prefer quiet, inline feedback to blocking windows.
- Failure: nags, "what's new" popups mid-task, badges that never clear, attention-grabbing motion on idle elements, sounds and vibration nobody asked for.

### 6. Good design is honest
Rams: it does not make a product appear more innovative, powerful or valuable than it is, and it does not manipulate with promises it cannot keep.
- Controls do exactly what they say. Progress shown is real progress. Leaving is as easy as joining.
- People stay in control of their time and attention, in apps and games alike.
- Failure: dark patterns of any kind (confirmshaming, hidden costs, disguised ads, flows that are easy to enter and hard to leave, pre-selected choices that serve the maker), engagement traps, streak pressure, fake scarcity, artificial urgency.

### 7. Good design is long-lasting
Rams: it avoids being fashionable and therefore never appears antiquated.
- What people have learned is an investment; keep layouts, inputs and terms stable, and help people across when you must change them.
- Failure: redesigns for their own sake, moved controls after an update, lost saves or settings.

### 8. Good design is thorough down to the last detail
Rams: nothing must be arbitrary or left to chance; care and accuracy show respect for the user.
- The details people feel: where focus lands, what happens after an error, whether input and position survive going back, whether they can pick up again after an interruption.
- Failure: states nobody designed (empty, failed, too much), focus lost after a dialog, a form that forgets what was typed, a voice or pen action with no way to undo.

### 9. Good design is environmentally friendly
Rams: design conserves resources and minimizes physical and visual pollution over the product's life.
- For operation this means respecting people's resources: their time, attention and energy first, then their device's battery, memory and bandwidth.
- Failure: visual noise, background activity that drains the device, waste people pay for without benefit.

### 10. Good design is as little design as possible
Rams: less, but better, because it concentrates on the essentials and doesn't burden the product with non-essentials. "Back to purity, back to simplicity."
- Remove before you add. Variety that only adds things to remember, without new meaningful choices, is not depth.
- Failure: a maze of settings instead of good defaults; extra paths that do subtly different things; labels and symbols that say the same thing twice. A faster path beside a findable one is not this failure.
